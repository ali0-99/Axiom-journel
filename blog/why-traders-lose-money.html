Why 90% of Traders Lose Money (And How to Be in the Other 10%)

By Ali Raza · Axiom Trading Journal

It's the statistic every trader has heard and every trader assumes doesn't apply to them: most retail traders lose money, and most of them don't lose it because of a bad strategy. They lose it because of what they do after the strategy stops working the way they expected. The gap between the losing majority and the profitable minority is smaller — and more fixable — than it looks.

A strategy with a 40% win rate and a 2:1 reward-to-risk ratio is profitable over 100 trades. The same strategy, traded by someone who abandons it after five losses in a row, is not. The strategy was never the problem.

01 They Trade Without a Written Plan

Without a plan written down before the trade, every decision gets made in the moment — under stress, with money already at risk, which is the worst possible time to be objective. Traders with a losing streak and no written plan will quietly shift their own rules mid-trade without realizing it, moving a stop loss "just a little," or holding past a target because "it feels like it's still going." A plan written down before entry removes that improvisation.

02 They Risk Too Much Per Trade

Risking 5-10% of an account on a single trade feels fine when it wins, and account-ending when a short losing streak arrives — and every strategy has losing streaks, including good ones. Risking 1-2% per trade means a string of five or six losses in a row barely dents the account, which keeps a trader in the game long enough for their edge to actually play out over a large enough sample.

03 They Move Their Stop Loss

A stop loss moved further away "to give the trade room to breathe" is rarely a technical decision — it's usually the fear of being wrong, disguised as strategy. This single habit turns small, planned losses into large, unplanned ones, and is one of the fastest ways a profitable-on-paper strategy becomes unprofitable in practice.

04 They Trade to Win Back a Loss

Revenge trading — entering a new trade immediately after a loss, often larger than usual, purely to "get the money back" — replaces a trader's actual strategy with pure emotion for the rest of that session. The trades taken in this state almost never follow the trader's normal setup criteria, because the goal has quietly shifted from "find a good trade" to "feel okay again."

05 They Overtrade in Choppy Markets

Not every session offers a valid setup, but sitting on hands feels unproductive, so traders take marginal trades just to be doing something. Most strategies are built around specific market conditions, and forcing trades outside those conditions is effectively trading with no edge at all — the losses that come from this are structural, not bad luck.

06 They Never Review Their Own Trades

Without a record of what was actually done — not what a trader remembers doing, but the entry, exit, size, and reasoning at the time — the same mistakes repeat indefinitely, because there's nothing forcing them into view. Traders who review a written log regularly tend to catch a pattern like "I lose more on trades I take after 2pm" or "I risk more when I've just had a win" — patterns that are invisible without the data.

07 They Confuse Being Right With Being Profitable

A high win rate feels good and proves very little on its own — a strategy that wins 80% of the time but risks 3x its average win on the losing 20% is a losing strategy overall. The traders who make it into the profitable minority tend to think in terms of expectancy across many trades, not whether any single trade was "right."
